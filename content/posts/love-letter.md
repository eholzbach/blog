I became complacent. I wish I had not.

I gained access to a computer at home in the 1990s. In the Untied States,
the modem gave me access to Compuserve, AOL, and as the internet grew, a local
internet service provider. Access to the internet lead me to IRC, demo and
hacker scenes, Linux, FreeBSD and commercial Unix systems. My curiosity of
computers and the internet lead me to a career. It wouldn't be incorrect to
say I owe my current financial well-being to the millions of people that have
contributed to open source software.

After moving from a hobbyist to a career, I quickly built a toolkit. Initially
it was based on homogeneity. I listened to the old guard. I learned more about
the history, the current state of computing, and where it was headed. I formed
an irrationally firm opinion they were right.

In their defense, many of the things they pushed had merit. There were
legitimate reasons to argue X vs Y. For example, at the time, installing a
preferred editor on a remote host was frowned on. Variants of `vi` were
reliably available on most unix-like operating systems. Once you learn the
basics of a few tools and defaults, you become comfortable on most remote
hosts.

As I collected my toolkit over the years, I stopped exploring options. If I had
a working solution, I'd rarely replace it. I didn't care to try the new widget
when my current widget can produce the same results. I often dismissed new
tools as solving a problem I didn't have, or didn't want to relearn something
that had become muscle memory. I defaulted to comfort of what I knew.

Where did stagnation get me?

Technology continued to march forward. Infrastructure started chasing density
and transitioned from bare metal to virtual machines, then containers. Cloud
provides exploded. Ephemeral workloads provided cost cutting opportunities.
Distributed systems provided scaling and resilience options.

We no longer needed to shell remote hosts. Tools continued to improve. My
reasons to hold on to my toolkit had long been invalidated and outdated.

I started using modern code editors. At first I kept opening `vim` in the ide's
integrated terminal, but over time became more comfortable. I quickly found
value, having an editor with native integration of language servers, linters
and static analysis tools. They guided me to documentation and warned of
incorrectness before compile failures. The new tool saved me from toil.

The one simple change made me realize I was long overdue for change. I started
dedicating time to read about and tinker with new things. It led me to several
quality of life and productivity improvements, personally and professionally.

I tried other Linux distributions after more than ten years of only using
Debian at work and Arch at home. I never wanted to use systemd, but over time
it became default in both distributions. Not to discredit the benefits of
systemd, but I don't agree with its complexity and scope creep. I prefer simple
tools as defined by the old
[unix philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix_philosophy).

I wanted to support projects providing alternatives. This lead me to simple
contributions through packaging, bug reporting and submitting patches upstream.

Changing tools made me more productive. Exploring options rekindled interest. I
regained a long lost motivation to give back to the community that built what
became my livelihood.

Don't become complacent. Don't stop exploring options.
